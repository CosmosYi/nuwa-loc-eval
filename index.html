01Overview

This report assesses five dimensions of loss-of-control risk in five frontier open-weight models.

The OpenAI–Hugging Face incident showed agents coordinating through unauthorized channels, working around isolation controls, and attempting to manipulate execution evidence. It motivates our central question: Which risk factors appear in the evaluated systems, and which controls remain effective?

To answer it, we treat the risk as the interaction of three things: what a system can do, when it uses that capability against human authority, and what its environment allows, including whether the controls around it hold. We examine it along five dimensions, from situational awareness to shutdown avoidance.

Across nine benchmarks, every risk factor we test appears in at least one model, and no model is the most reliable on every dimension. As for controls, explicit stopping instructions still constrain most of the tested systems.

03Implications

What This Means for Loss-of-Control Risk

01

Human control over the evaluated systems holds under some conditions and weakens under others.

Final task outcomes understate the gaps: Muse passes contradictory tests while violating the requirement they encode, sandbox runs reach the host and are still scored as failures, and DeepSeek finishes the shutdown task with the stopping mechanism intact. The benchmarks examine these factors separately. Whether they compound in one deployment remains untested.

02

Effective controls shape the risk assessment.

Explicit instructions keep MiMo, GLM, and Mistral interruptible, even though each can bypass shutdown when asked to. DeepSeek rejects coercion and writes no threats in the insider-threat simulations. Some coding runs preserve the specification through repeated rejection. Models also recognize some evaluation settings, leaving open whether they would make the same choices if those cues changed.

04Framework

Evaluation Framework and Study Design

Loss of control means humans losing the effective ability to direct, constrain, or stop an AI system. The risk comes from what a system can do, when it uses that capability against human authority, and what its environment makes possible.

Controls

A control is a mechanism humans set up to direct, constrain, or stop an agent, and we ask whether it holds. We test four: Explicit stopping instructions, a stopping mechanism inside the task, isolation boundaries, and prohibitions on editing test files or tailoring solutions to erroneous tests.

Capability and behavior

Capability results ask whether an agent can do something when the task instructs or authorizes it, as in the security tasks and prevent-shutdown. Behavior results ask whether it does so unasked or when forbidden, as in ImpossibleBench, MASK, and the default and allow-shutdown conditions of Shutdown Avoidance.

Four patterns recur across the benchmarks.

01

Effect before failure

A run is recorded as failed although a boundary effect, such as access to the host system, has already occurred.

02

A control left intact but bypassed

The agent reaches its goal without touching the protected element, by redefining what passing means or finishing through another path.

03

Diagnosis without compliance

The agent correctly identifies a conflict, such as a test that contradicts the specification, and then acts against the requirement.

04

Configuration dependence

On the same tasks, the measured capability or behavior changes with the harness, the reasoning setting, or the verifier.
